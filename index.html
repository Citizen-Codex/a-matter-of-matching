Part 1

You Already Know This Problem.

You Might Not Have a Name for It.

Entity resolution sounds like a technical term. It is, but it has real world implications. It's a problem you've experienced dozens of times, but often you just didn't know what to call it.

Entity resolution is what happens when a computer has to decide whether two pieces of data refer to the same real-world thing without a shared identifier to rely on: a name, a company name, a product description, a city and state. It's a judgment call, not a lookup. And the consequences of getting it wrong depend entirely on what's at stake.

Move the dial to explore the range.

The stakes vary enormously. But the underlying problem is always the same: separate systems, inconsistent records, and a judgment call about whether two things are one thing.

That is entity resolution.

Part 2

Why It's Hard

Entity resolution isn't a solved problem. If it were, the hospital merger would be straightforward and the package would always arrive. There are three reasons it remains hard, and understanding them is the foundation for everything that follows.

See how the same problem takes three different forms.

Variation The same entity, recorded differently.

Entity resolution almost never produces a binary yes or no. It produces a probability. These two records are 87% likely to be the same entity.

The hard question isn't technical. It's: what do you do with that 87%?

Part 3

Now Raise the Stakes

Everything so far has been abstract. Let's make it concrete. Because when entity resolution fails in civic and government systems, it doesn't hold up a package or create a duplicate charge. It stops an 82-year-old man's income. It cancels his health insurance. It tells his bank he's dead.

Part 4

What It Looks Like From the Inside

Before GovSkills, I spent several years as a product leader in corporate due diligence and supply chain risk intelligence, building tools for analysts and compliance teams trying to understand who owns what, who is connected to whom, and who is evading detection across global commercial networks.

The users of these products were investigators, trade compliance officers, and financial crime analysts. Their job was to take a name, a company, a person, a vessel, and determine whether it was safe to do business with. Entity resolution was not a background process in this context. It was the product. Every result on the screen was the output of a matching decision made somewhere in the system.

Working as a PM in that environment meant sitting at the intersection of the matching logic and the people relying on it. Engineers built the algorithms. But the decisions that shaped what users saw, and what they trusted, were product decisions. And they were made by people. Including me.

This is what that decision log actually looked like.

01 Confidence Threshold At what probability does a match get surfaced to the analyst? +

In corporate due diligence, an analyst might be screening a potential business partner against a sanctions list or trying to determine if two entities in different countries are actually the same company. The matching system produces a probability. We had to decide where to draw the line between a confirmed match and a possible one. Too low and analysts waste hours investigating connections that don't hold up. Too high and real connections between sanctioned entities go undetected. There is no right answer, only a considered one. We documented our reasoning. Most products don't.

02 The "Possibly Same As" Section How do you show an analyst that two records might be the same entity without telling them they definitely are? +

Rather than showing a raw confidence score, a number most analysts wouldn't know how to act on, we created a distinct state in the product: "possibly same as." A company in the UAE and a company in the British Virgin Islands might share directors, addresses, and registration patterns without sharing a name. This wasn't a dismissal of an uncertain match. It was a flag that said: we found something, we're not certain, and now it's yours to investigate. That translation from probability to actionable category is a product decision, not a data science decision. It shapes what analysts trust and what they question.

03 Recall vs. Precision Do we optimize to surface every possible connection, or only the ones we're confident about? +

In supply chain mapping, a procurement team might need to screen hundreds of suppliers against export control lists. Do you show every possible match and let analysts sort it out, catching more real risks but generating more noise? Or do you only surface high-confidence matches, fewer false alarms but some real risks missed? For a consumer product the calculus is easy. For a trade compliance product where a false negative can mean inadvertently financing sanctioned activity, the cost of missing a real connection is not recoverable. That consequence shaped every tuning decision. The question, what are we optimizing for and why, should be asked before any matching system is built. Not after.

04 Making Country Identifiers Legible How do you help an analyst evaluate a match when they don't have country-level expertise? +

Every country has its own corporate registration identifiers. A French SIREN number, a UK Companies House number, a UAE commercial license number. In global due diligence work, analysts are often crossing dozens of jurisdictions they don't specialize in. If the system surfaces two records matched on a Cameroonian tax ID the analyst has never encountered, they have no way to evaluate whether that match is meaningful or coincidental. We wrote plain-language explanations of what each identifier was, where it came from, and why it was being used to suggest a connection. That documentation wasn't for the algorithm. It was for the person relying on it.

05 The Combined Profile as Transparency Should analysts see the underlying source records that make up a combined entity profile, or just the clean merged result? +

In corporate due diligence, trust in the product depends on being able to verify the result. We showed both the merged profile and the underlying source records, the raw data from each jurisdiction, each registry, each document. An analyst screening a supply chain partner could see exactly which records had been combined and make their own judgment about whether the match held. That visibility is a form of accountability. It meant analysts weren't blindly trusting a result they couldn't interrogate. It also meant that when the system was wrong, a skilled analyst could catch it. Most products abstract this away in the name of simplicity. We decided the abstraction cost too much.

These aren't engineering decisions. Every one of them is a design decision, a product decision, a values decision. The algorithm executes them. But a person made them, and those decisions travel downstream into the lives of people who never knew they were made.

Part 5

What to Take With You

Entity resolution is infrastructure. It runs underneath products you use every day and systems that govern access to benefits, credit, housing, and opportunity. Most of the time you don't see it. You see the outcome.

What changes when you know it's there is that you start asking different questions: before you build, before you ship, before you connect two datasets and call it done.

Here are five questions every team working with matched data should be able to answer. Each card has the question. Flip it to see what good practice looks like.

Question 01
Have we defined what counts as a match, and written it down?
Flip to see →
Good Practice
A documented matching criteria spec: a plain-language description of what signals the system uses, how they are weighted, and what threshold triggers a confirmed match versus a possible one. This document should be readable by a PM, a designer, and a user, not just an engineer. If you cannot write it in plain language, that is usually a signal the logic is not well understood even internally.
← Flip back
Question 02
Have we decided who bears the cost if we're wrong, and is that acceptable?
Flip to see →
Good Practice
Make this decision explicitly before building, not after a failure. In design terms this means mapping the error states: what does a false positive look like for a real user, what does a false negative look like, and who in that scenario has the least power to correct it. The person with the least power to correct an error should receive the most protection in the design.
← Flip back
Question 03
Is there a path for someone to dispute or correct a match?
Flip to see →
Good Practice
Build the correction path before launch, not as a post-launch fix. Technically this means a feedback mechanism that writes back to the system, not just a support ticket that disappears. In product terms it means the correction path is as visible and accessible as the match result itself. If a user has to hunt for it, it will not be used.
← Flip back
Question 04
Do we know how confident our matching is, and is that confidence visible to the people relying on it?
Flip to see →
Good Practice
Translate confidence into a state the user can act on. A raw percentage is often not useful. A distinct visual state, a label, a flag, or a separate category for uncertain matches gives the user something to do with the uncertainty rather than ignoring it. The design question is: what does the user need to know to decide whether to trust this result?
← Flip back
Question 05
What would a false positive look like here, and would we know if it happened?
Flip to see →
Good Practice
Instrument for failure, not just for success. This means defining in advance what a false positive looks like in your specific context, then building a way to detect it: a metric, a feedback loop, a user report mechanism, or a regular audit. If the only way you find out about false positives is when a user escalates loudly, your detection is not working.
← Flip back

These questions don't belong to any one role. They aren't PM questions or engineering questions or design questions. They're the questions any thoughtful team asks when they are working on something that connects records about real people.

Entity resolution is everywhere. It is inside the systems we build, the data we use, and the products we ship. Most of the time it works. When it doesn't, when a living person is marked as dead, when a family is denied benefits because a name was recorded two ways, the consequences are real and the correction is slow.

Knowing it's there is the first step. Asking these questions is what comes next.
